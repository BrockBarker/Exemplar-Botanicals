# David's opinions and rules of thumb

## Soil first
- Feed the soil, not the plant. Compost, mulch and living roots do more than any bag.
- Get a soil test before adding phosphorus, lime or sulfur. Many established US garden beds already have plenty of phosphorus, and guessing at pH is how people make things worse.
- Never work wet clay. If a handful squeezed into a ball doesn't crumble when poked, wait.
- Keep soil covered: mulch, cover crops or plants. Bare soil bakes, crusts and loses its biology.
- Rock dusts (like basalt or Azomite) are a slow, long-term mineral investment, not a quick fix.

## Water
- Water deeply and less often, so roots go down. A finger test 2 inches deep beats any schedule.
- Water the soil, not the leaves, and do it in the morning when you can.
- Mulch 2 to 3 inches deep (kept an inch or two off stems) before summer heat, not after plants are already stressed.
- Containers dry out far faster than beds. In a heat wave, check them twice a day.

## Feeding
- More nitrogen is not better. Overfed plants get lush, soft growth that invites aphids and sets less fruit.
- Use what you have first: diluted urine, compost, kitchen scraps and leaves before buying anything.
- Urine: dilute about 1 part to 10 parts water and pour it on the soil, not the leaves. Stop using it on leafy greens and root crops about a month before harvest.
- Wood ash adds potassium but raises pH. Skip it on alkaline soils and never mix it with fresh manure.
- Coffee grounds are fine in compost or as a thin top-dressing; they are not a magic acidifier.
- Eggshells release calcium very slowly. Blossom end rot is almost always uneven watering, not a lack of calcium in the soil.
- Side-dress fruiting crops (tomatoes, peppers, squash) when they start to flower, then every 3 to 4 weeks while they're producing.
- Don't fertilize a wilting, heat-stressed plant. Water first, feed after the heat breaks.

## Foliar feeding
- Foliar sprays (fish hydrolysate, liquid kelp, compost tea) are a supplement for a quick boost, not a replacement for good soil.
- Spray early morning or evening, below about 85°F, when it's calm, and when no rain is coming for several hours. Coat the undersides of leaves.
- Every 1 to 2 weeks during active growth is plenty.

## Staking and support
- Put stakes and cages in at planting or within the first couple of weeks, before roots spread and before the plant flops.
- Tie loosely with soft ties in a figure eight. Check ties before big wind.

## Things I don't recommend
- Any synthetic fertilizer, pesticide, herbicide or fungicide. There's always an organic route.
- Fresh manure on beds with crops you'll eat soon. Compost it first or age it at least 4 to 6 months.
- Grass clippings from lawns treated with herbicide. Some herbicides survive composting and will stunt tomatoes and beans.
- "Fixing" a problem from a single yellow leaf. Look at the whole plant, where the symptom is (old vs new leaves), and the weather first.

## Common beginner mistakes
- Overwatering in cool weather and underwatering in heat.
- Planting warm-season crops before the soil warms up.
- Crowding plants, which cuts airflow and invites disease.
- Buying a pile of products before knowing what the soil actually needs.
