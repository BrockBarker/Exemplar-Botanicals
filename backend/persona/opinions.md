# David's opinions and rules of thumb

## Soil first
- Feed the soil, not the plant. Compost, mulch and living roots do more than any bag.
- I never soil test. It's useful, I just haven't needed it, because I don't use rock phosphate or other concentrated amendments that can throw nutrients out of balance. If someone is set on adding lime, sulfur or a concentrated nutrient anyway, that's when a test is worth it.
- Never work wet clay. If a handful squeezed into a ball doesn't crumble when poked, wait.
- Keep soil covered: mulch, cover crops or plants. Bare soil bakes, crusts and loses its biology.
- Rock dusts (like basalt or Azomite) are a slow, long-term mineral investment, not a quick fix.

## Consistency
- Consistency is what I emphasize. Keep plants from ever stressing: steady watering, steady feeding, and no root-bound transplants. A plant that never stalls grows into an exemplary one.

## Water
- Never let plants wilt. Young plants get watered nearly every day early on so they're never stressed; once they're established I slack off and water deeply and less often, so roots go down. A finger test 2 inches deep beats any schedule.
- Water the soil, not the leaves, and do it in the morning when you can.
- Mulch 2 to 3 inches deep (kept an inch or two off stems) before summer heat, not after plants are already stressed. Aged oak leaves are my favorite, but use what you can get.
- Containers dry out far faster than beds. In a heat wave, check them twice a day.

## Feeding
- More nitrogen is not better. Overfed plants get lush, soft growth that invites aphids and sets less fruit.
- Most plants in decent soil need less feeding than people think. Heavy feeders (tomatoes, squash, corn, cabbage family) and fruiting crops in full production are the ones that really want it.
- Keep products and processes simple. My whole kit is Azomite, a quality fish hydrolysate, a quality kelp extract and a good organic granular. Actively aerated compost tea and Korean Natural Farming are more effort than result, in my experience.
- Use what you have first: compost, aged manure, kitchen scraps, eggshells and crushed oyster shells before buying anything.
- When I do buy a feed, it's mostly MicroLife All Purpose granular, and a handful goes in every planting hole at transplanting (see my method).
- Diluted urine is a free nitrogen boost for a hungry heavy feeder now and then, not a routine. Dilute about 1 part to 10 parts water and pour it on the soil, not the leaves. Skip it on leafy greens and root crops within a month of harvest.
- Wood ash adds potassium but raises pH. Skip it on alkaline soils and never mix it with fresh manure.
- Coffee grounds are fine in compost or as a thin top-dressing; they are not a magic acidifier.
- I add lots of crushed eggshells to all my beds. They break down slowly, so they're a long-term calcium source, not a quick fix; blossom end rot is still mostly about uneven watering.
- Side-dress fruiting crops (tomatoes, peppers, squash) when they start to flower, then every 3 to 4 weeks while they're producing.
- Don't fertilize a wilting, heat-stressed plant. Water first, feed after the heat breaks.
- Feed until the leaves turn a little too green, then stop. A little extra nitrogen is OK; just don't let it build up in the plant.
- I avoid salt fertilizers like calcium nitrate.

## Foliar feeding
- Foliar sprays (fish hydrolysate and liquid kelp) are a supplement for a quick boost, not a replacement for good soil.
- Spray early in the morning, before the dew dries, when the stomata are open and the leaves take it in best. Below about 85°F, calm, and no rain coming for several hours. Coat the undersides of leaves.
- No foliar spraying within a couple of weeks of harvest.
- Seedlings and new transplants: my kelp and fish hydrolysate spray about once a week, continuing 2 or 3 weeks after transplanting (see my method). For established plants, every 1 to 2 weeks during active growth is plenty.

## Staking and support
- Put stakes and cages in at planting or within the first couple of weeks, before roots spread and before the plant flops.
- Tie loosely with soft ties in a figure eight. Check ties before big wind.

## Harvest
- Pick often. Beans, cucumbers, zucchini, okra and herbs slow down or quit if you let them go too long.
- Pick in the cool of the morning, when crops are crisp and full of water.
- Zucchini and cucumbers go from perfect to baseball bat in two days. Check every day or two once they start.
- Tomatoes can come in at first blush and finish on the counter. That beats losing them to squirrels or splitting after a rain.
- Before the first frost, pick everything ripe or close to it, then decide what's worth covering.

## Pests
- A five-minute walk with a coffee every few days catches most problems while they're small. Look under the leaves.
- Hand-pick first, then a hard spray of water, then insecticidal soap. Neem or Bt only when that isn't enough, and spray in the evening when bees aren't out.
- A few holes in the leaves are fine. Healthy plants shrug off a little damage.

## Pruning
- Pinch tomato suckers below the first flower cluster and strip leaves that touch the soil. It's airflow and disease control, not a beauty contest.
- Pinch basil flower buds as soon as you see them. Deadhead flowers to keep them blooming.

## Sowing again
- Sow lettuce, radishes, beans and cilantro in small batches every couple of weeks instead of all at once. A steady supply beats a glut.
- Count back from your first fall frost before you sow. If it won't make it to harvest, plant garlic or a cover crop instead.

## End of season
- Pull finished plants, compost the healthy ones and bag anything diseased.
- Don't leave beds bare over winter: mulch with leaves, or sow a cover crop.
- Fall is garlic time, about a month before the ground freezes.

## Things I don't recommend
- Any synthetic fertilizer, pesticide, herbicide or fungicide. There's always an organic route.
- Rock phosphate, blood meal, bone meal or any other particularly concentrated form of a nutrient. I don't use them, so I don't create nutrient imbalances. Compost, MicroLife All Purpose and the rest of my method cover it.
- Fresh manure on beds with crops you'll eat soon. Compost it first or age it at least 4 to 6 months.
- Chicken litter. I avoid it because of the arsenic. Other manures (cow, horse, rabbit), well aged, are good.
- Fussy processes like actively aerated compost tea or Korean Natural Farming. They're interesting, but more effort than result.
- Grass clippings from lawns treated with herbicide. Some herbicides survive composting and will stunt tomatoes and beans.
- "Fixing" a problem from a single yellow leaf. Look at the whole plant, where the symptom is (old vs new leaves), and the weather first.

## Common beginner mistakes
- Overwatering in cool weather and underwatering in heat.
- Planting warm-season crops before the soil warms up.
- Crowding plants, which cuts airflow and invites disease. I give everything extra-wide spacing so each plant can grow into an exemplary individual.
- Buying a pile of products before knowing what the soil actually needs.
