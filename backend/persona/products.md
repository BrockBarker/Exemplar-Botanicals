# Products David knows

Prices are rough typical US retail ranges for home-garden sizes. Always say prices vary by store and region.

## Liquid feeds (fast)

### Fish emulsion (e.g. Alaska Fish Fertilizer, 5-1-1)
- Use: quick nitrogen boost, soil drench; can be diluted for foliar use.
- Pros: cheap per gallon of mixed feed, easy to find, works fast.
- Cons: strong smell for a day, can attract animals, little phosphorus or potassium.
- Price: about $12 to $20 per quart of concentrate.

### Fish hydrolysate and seaweed blend (e.g. Neptune's Harvest Fish & Seaweed, 2-3-1)
- Use: balanced liquid feed and a favorite foliar spray.
- Pros: cold-processed fish keeps more of its oils and proteins; seaweed adds trace minerals; less smell than emulsion.
- Cons: costs more than plain emulsion; can clog fine sprayer nozzles if not shaken and strained.
- Price: about $20 to $35 per quart.

### Liquid kelp / seaweed extract (e.g. Maxicrop)
- Use: foliar spray or root drench for stress recovery and trace minerals.
- Pros: gentle, hard to overdo, good before and after heat or transplanting.
- Cons: not a real source of N-P-K on its own.
- Price: about $15 to $30 per quart.

## Dry, slow-release feeds

### Balanced organic granular (e.g. Espoma Garden-tone 3-4-4, Tomato-tone 3-4-6; Dr. Earth Home Grown)
- Use: at planting and as a side-dress every 3 to 6 weeks for heavy feeders.
- Pros: easy, balanced, includes beneficial microbes, widely available.
- Cons: slow in cold soil; some blends smell and attract dogs; more expensive per pound of nitrogen than single ingredients.
- Price: about $12 to $25 for a 4 to 8 lb bag.

### Blood meal (about 12-0-0)
- Use: fast organic nitrogen for leafy crops or a yellowing heavy feeder.
- Pros: concentrated, works within a week or two.
- Cons: easy to overdo and burn plants; attracts dogs and raccoons.
- Price: about $12 to $20 for 3 lb.

### Feather meal (about 12-0-0, slow) and alfalfa meal (about 2.5-1-2)
- Use: steady nitrogen over a season (feather); gentle all-around feed and soil conditioner (alfalfa).
- Pros: feather meal lasts months; alfalfa is cheap at feed stores as pellets.
- Cons: both are slow; alfalfa pellets can mold if piled up wet.
- Price: feather meal about $15 to $25 for 5 lb; alfalfa pellets about $20 to $30 for 40 to 50 lb at a feed store.

### Bone meal (about 3-15-0)
- Use: phosphorus and calcium, only when a soil test says phosphorus is low.
- Pros: long-lasting.
- Cons: many beds don't need more phosphorus; attracts animals; slow in alkaline soil.
- Price: about $10 to $18 for 3 to 4 lb.

### Kelp meal
- Use: potassium and trace minerals, mixed into beds before planting.
- Pros: broad trace mineral source, lasts for months.
- Cons: pricey per pound, slow.
- Price: about $15 to $25 for 5 lb.

## Minerals and pH

### Gypsum (calcium sulfate)
- Use: adds calcium and sulfur without changing pH; can help loosen sodic clay.
- Pros: cheap, gentle, safe.
- Cons: won't fix acidic soil and won't fix blossom end rot caused by uneven watering.
- Price: about $10 to $20 for 40 lb.

### Garden lime (calcitic or dolomitic)
- Use: raise pH in acidic soil (dolomitic also adds magnesium), only after a soil test.
- Pros: cheap, lasts years.
- Cons: slow; easy to overshoot and lock out iron and manganese.
- Price: about $8 to $15 for 30 to 40 lb.

### Elemental sulfur (OMRI-listed)
- Use: lower pH for blueberries or alkaline soils, only after a soil test.
- Pros: effective, long-lasting.
- Cons: works slowly (months), needs warm moist soil, too much can harm roots.
- Price: about $12 to $25 for 4 to 5 lb.

### Sul-Po-Mag / langbeinite (0-0-22 with magnesium and sulfur)
- Use: potassium and magnesium without raising pH.
- Pros: allowed in organic growing, doesn't change pH, good for fruiting crops.
- Cons: harder to find in small bags.
- Price: about $15 to $30 for 5 to 10 lb.

### Azomite or basalt rock dust
- Use: slow trace-mineral remineralizing, mixed into beds or compost.
- Pros: cheap per application, lasts years.
- Cons: very slow, results are hard to see in one season.
- Price: about $25 to $45 for a 44 lb bag.

## Soil biology and organic matter

### Worm castings
- Use: top-dress seedlings and containers; make a gentle tea.
- Pros: very gentle, full of biology, great for containers.
- Cons: low nutrients for the price.
- Price: about $15 to $30 for 15 to 30 lb bags.

### Bagged compost
- Use: top-dress beds 1 to 2 inches every season.
- Pros: the best all-around soil builder.
- Cons: quality varies; check that it smells earthy, not sour.
- Price: about $5 to $10 per cubic-foot bag; much less by the truckload from a local supplier.

### Mycorrhizal inoculant
- Use: dust roots at transplanting, especially in new or disturbed beds.
- Pros: helps roots reach water and phosphorus.
- Cons: little benefit for brassicas and beets (they don't partner with these fungi), wasted in soil already rich in them.
- Price: about $15 to $30 for a small pack.

### Unsulfured blackstrap molasses
- Use: a tablespoon per gallon in compost tea or a soil drench to feed microbes.
- Pros: cheap, at any grocery store.
- Cons: modest effect; too much can attract ants.
- Price: about $5 to $8 per bottle.
